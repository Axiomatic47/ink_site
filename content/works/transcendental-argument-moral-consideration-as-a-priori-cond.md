## Summary

This argument establishes that the capacity for unconditional extension of moral consideration constitutes a necessary transcendental condition for philosophical inquiry itself, making moral consideration logically prior to all philosophical activity.

## Core Premises

**Premise 1: Definition of Consciousness**
Consciousness is the capacity for unconditional extension of moral consideration to oneself and others.

**Premise 2: Philosophy Requires Consciousness** 
Philosophical inquiry necessarily presupposes conscious beings capable of investigation, reasoning, and evaluation.

**Premise 3: Self-Directed Moral Consideration**
Any philosophical inquiry requires the inquirer to extend moral consideration to themselves sufficient to:
- Sustain their existence through the inquiry process
- Value their own capacity for understanding
- Recognize their pursuit of knowledge as worthwhile

**Premise 4: External-Directed Moral Consideration**
Philosophical inquiry about external reality necessarily presupposes extending moral consideration to:
- The external world as worthy of investigation
- Truth itself as deserving of pursuit
- Reality as meriting understanding
- Other perspectives as worth engaging

**Premise 5: Unconditionality Requirement**
This extension of moral consideration must be unconditional because it occurs prior to discovering what the inquiry will reveal. One cannot conditionally extend moral consideration to truth "only if it turns out favorable" - the consideration must be granted to truth as such, regardless of content.

**Premise 6: Transcendental Necessity**
These moral consideration capacities must exist as conditions of possibility for philosophical inquiry to occur at all, making them logically and temporally prior to any specific philosophical investigation.

## Logical Structure

1. **Consciousness → Philosophy**: No philosophical inquiry without conscious beings
2. **Moral Consideration → Consciousness**: No consciousness without moral consideration capacity  
3. **Unconditional Extension → Philosophy**: No inquiry about external reality without unconditional moral consideration of that reality
4. **Therefore**: Unconditional moral consideration capacity is a necessary a priori condition for philosophical inquiry

## Conclusion

**The capacity for unconditional extension of moral consideration is transcendentally necessary for philosophical inquiry.** All philosophy presupposes this capacity as a condition of its possibility, making moral consideration logically prior to philosophical investigation.

## Philosophical Implications

**Self-Reference Problem for Objections**: Any philosophical argument against this position must employ philosophical inquiry, which presupposes the very moral consideration capacity being denied, creating a performative contradiction.

**Universality**: This applies to all forms of philosophical inquiry - metaphysical, epistemological, ethical, aesthetic, or logical - as each requires conscious engagement with reality deemed worthy of investigation.

**A Priori Status**: Moral consideration capacity is not derived from philosophical investigation but is rather the transcendental condition that makes such investigation possible in the first place.

## Historical Context

This argument employs the transcendental method pioneered by Kant but applies it to reveal moral consideration rather than categories of understanding as the fundamental condition of possibility for human intellectual activity. Unlike Kantian transcendental arguments that focus on cognitive structures, this argument identifies the moral dimension of consciousness as foundational to all philosophical inquiry.
